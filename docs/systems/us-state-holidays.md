# The US states' own holidays

The United States has federal holidays and fifty-one sets of state ones.
This document covers the days the states' codes list beyond the federal
list, which `hc-holiday`'s `UNITED_STATES` table carries as rules scoped
to each state's ISO 3166-2 code, and the District of Columbia's.

## What it is

5 U.S.C. § 6103 lists the eleven federal holidays. They bind the federal
government's employees and, by the custom of banks and markets, set much
of the country's calendar, but they bind no private employer and no
state. Each state's code has its own list of legal holidays. Most repeat
the federal days, some under other names (Washington-Lincoln Day,
Indigenous Peoples' Day, Frances Xavier Cabrini Day), some leave one out
(Columbus Day and Juneteenth most often), and many add days of their own:
a state's founding (Alaska Day, Nevada Day, West Virginia Day, Statehood
Day in Hawaii), a battle (San Jacinto, Bennington), a person (Seward,
Kuhio, Kamehameha, Truman), the Friday after Thanksgiving, Good Friday,
the Confederate days of the South, election days.

What a state's legal holiday does is the state's own affair, and it
varies. In some codes it closes the state's offices and gives its
employees a paid day (Alabama's "official state holidays", Nevada Day,
Texas's state holidays). In others it is a legal holiday for courts,
deadlines and negotiable instruments while the offices stay open, and a
separate personnel law says which days the employees have (Florida's
§ 683.01 against § 110.117, Maryland's General Provisions against its
State Personnel and Pensions article, Kentucky's "may be closed").
Besides the holidays, most codes designate commemorative days, often
dozens, which close nothing.

## How it works

Nevada's NRS 236.015 makes "the last Friday in October (Nevada Day)" a
legal holiday and says that all state, county and city offices, courts,
public schools and the Nevada System of Higher Education must close on
it [us-nv-code]. In 2026 the last Friday of October is the 30th. So
`UNITED_STATES` asked for `US-NV` in 2026 gives Nevada Day on Friday
30 October 2026 as `Kind::Government`; asked for `US-CA` or nationwide,
it does not. The day remains a business day: a private employer in Reno
owes nothing on it, and the table's business-day arithmetic counts only
the federal days off.

Florida's § 683.01 makes Lincoln's Birthday, 12 February, a "legal
holiday" and a "public holiday", but § 110.117, which lists the days the
state's offices close, does not have it [us-fl-code]. So Lincoln's
Birthday in `US-FL` is `Kind::Observance`, and the Friday after
Thanksgiving, which § 110.117 has, is `Kind::Government`.

## What is carried

- **The federal holidays**, nationwide, with Inauguration Day in `US-DC`.
- **The days each state's code lists beyond them**, scoped to its code,
  in the table below: 149 rules in 45 states and the District, and eight
  half days (`Kind::HalfDay`): California's Good Friday afternoon, Ohio's
  election-day afternoon, and the Saturdays of Michigan, New Jersey, New
  York, Ohio, Pennsylvania and Tennessee.
  - `Kind::Government` where the code, or the personnel law read beside
    it, closes the state's offices or makes the day a paid holiday for its
    employees, or where the code makes the day a legal holiday and says
    nothing to the contrary;
  - `Kind::Observance` where it is a legal holiday that closes nothing,
    or a day the code designates for observance. For the states whose
    legal-holiday list adds nothing, Idaho, Kansas, New Mexico, Oregon
    and South Dakota, the commemorative days of the holiday chapter read
    are carried in the table below.
- **The half days**, as `Kind::HalfDay`, from the year of the text read,
  a gap before. California's "Good Friday from 12 noon until 3 p.m."
  (§ 6700(a)(19)). And the Saturday half holidays, each Saturday a day of
  its own: Michigan's "Every Saturday from 12 noon until 12 midnight" (MCL
  435.101) and Pennsylvania's "every Saturday, after twelve o'clock noon
  until twelve o'clock midnight" (44 P.S. § 11), every Saturday of the
  year; New York's "noon to midnight of each Saturday which is not a
  public holiday" (Gen. Constr. Law § 24) and Tennessee's "each Saturday
  which is not a holiday" (§ 15-1-101), every Saturday but those on a
  dated holiday of the same section; Ohio's "Every Saturday afternoon"
  (R.C. 5.30), with its election day "between the hours of twelve noon
  ... and five-thirty p.m." (R.C. 5.20); and New Jersey's "every Saturday"
  (N.J.S.A. 36:1-1), a public holiday for instruments that "until
  12 o'clock noon" is "a secular or business day". A rule may yield 53
  days a year (`Days::CAPACITY`), the most Saturdays a year has.
- **The first year** of each day is the year a source read gives as the
  day's establishment, the session law that created it, where the
  section's history names one (South Dakota, Kansas, Idaho, Colorado,
  Oregon, California's Genocide Remembrance Day, Virginia's Election Day,
  Florida's Pascua Florida Day, the District); the years before are
  absent. Otherwise it is the year of the version read, the last
  amendment's where the history gives one and the copy's date where it does
  not, and the years before are a gap, which the engine reports for the
  rule read from that year (`HolidayRule::read_from`): the day is usually older (Texas's days were recodified in
  1993, Hawaii's are of the 1890s), and the texts that would show it were
  not read. The table's "First year" column says which.
- **Not yet carried, and why:**
  - the days the table's last column names: days whose date a governor
    chooses or an election law not read sets, days for a county or a
    parish, optional days, and days on the federal date under a state
    name;
  - Louisiana's Saturday half-holidays and whole Saturdays, which
    La. R.S. 1:55 sets parish by parish, finer than a state;
  - the states' weekend moves: many codes keep a Saturday day on the
    Friday and a Sunday day on the Monday, but the engine substitutes only
    days off, and a state's day is not one; the code's rule is noted;
  - New Hampshire and Oklahoma, whose codes could not be read (tried
    again on 2026-09-29: the General Court's site and OSCN refused the
    connection, FindLaw's and Justia's copies answered 403 or not found,
    the Internet Archive was offline, and Oklahoma's Title 25 is otherwise
    published as PDF), and Georgia,
    whose extra state holiday the Governor chooses each year: asked for,
    each keeps the federal days and reports its own as a gap. Wyoming,
    whose code adds no day, is listed among the subdivisions read and is
    complete;
  - the commemorative days the section "The commemorative days" lists as
    not carried: days a governor dates each year, days tied to an event
    the code does not date, and days whose wording leaves the date open.

### The commemorative days

Beside their legal holidays, most codes designate days of observance,
which close nothing: a person's day, an anniversary, an awareness day.
`crates/hc-holiday/src/countries/united_states/commemorative.rs` carries
1,322 of them in 45 states, each as `Kind::Observance` scoped to its
state and cited to the sections read: from the session law that set it
where the section's history names one, the years before absent, and
otherwise from the year of the version read, the years before a gap.
Read on 2026-09-29, each state's commemorative chapter or article in
full where the rows below say so, on the legislature's site where it
answered, and otherwise in the Internet Archive's capture of it or in
FindLaw's copy, secondary; FindLaw's copies are of 2024 to 2026 and may
lack the latest days. A day that shares a federal holiday's name but not
its date is carried under its own rule (Arkansas's Columbus Day on
12 October); a day on a federal holiday's date under another name, a
week or a month is not. The days a code designates without a date a
calendar rule can give are in the last column.

## Accuracy

`crates/hc-holiday/tests/us_states.rs` holds each of the 149 rules to
its code: the day's date in its first year and in 2026 (the election days
in their years), worked out from the code's rule by a script apart from
the crate; its kind and its state; nothing the year before, and a gap
there unless the first year is the day's establishment (Colorado's 2019
absent, Texas's 2024 a gap, Kansas's Native American Day absent in 1944
and a gap in 1945 to 2012); Texas
Independence Day in Texas alone and still a business day there; the
even-year and presidential election days; Utah's Monday Juneteenth;
Arizona's two Code Talkers days of 2026, Sunday 16 August and Friday
14 August; and the Saturday half holidays: 52 in Michigan and
Pennsylvania in 2026 and 53 in Michigan in 2033, New York's and
Tennessee's 51 in 2026 without Saturday 4 July, New York's 2022 without
1 January and 12 February, and New Jersey's 52 in 2024.
What a reader should know:

- Most copies read were secondary, and several official sites refused
  the connection or render by script; the instrument's entry says which.
  A secondary copy may lag the law: FindLaw's copies are of 2023 to 2026.
- The first years are mostly those of the text read, not of the day, so
  a calendar for 2020 in `US-TX` reports the Texas days as gaps rather
  than claiming there were none.
- The line between `Kind::Government` and `Kind::Observance` follows
  what the code read says the day does, and a personnel rule not read can
  close offices on a day the code calls a mere legal holiday, as North
  Carolina's State Human Resources Commission does.
- Arizona's Sunday holidays are carried because § 1-301 lists them; they
  close nothing that is open. National Navajo Code Talkers Day is in
  § 1-301's list, kept on the Sunday on or after 14 August (subsection E),
  while § 1-313 says that 14 August "shall be observed as Navajo code
  talkers' day" and that the day "is not a legal holiday". Neither section
  yields to the other, and the site read shows no history to say which is
  later, so both are carried: the holiday on its Sunday, and 14 August as
  an observance.
- Tennessee's half-holiday is "each Saturday which is not a holiday", and
  the same section keeps a holiday that falls on a Saturday on the Friday
  before. The table reads a Saturday that is one of the section's dated
  holidays (1 January, 19 June, 4 July, 11 November, 25 December) as a
  holiday still, and gives it no half-holiday; the other reading, that the
  move leaves the Saturday a half-holiday, is not carried. New York's § 24
  moves only a Sunday holiday, so its reading is plain.
- A half holiday is a Saturday's afternoon, and the table's entry is the
  whole Saturday; the hours are in the section quoted.
- The commemorative days rest on quotations checked against the raw text
  of the page where it was read as HTML (the legislature's sites, the
  Internet Archive's captures, FindLaw's pages read in a browser). For
  Indiana, Kentucky, Michigan, Mississippi, Nebraska, Nevada, New Jersey,
  North Dakota, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Utah and Wyoming, and four of
  Massachusetts's sections, the pages were first read through a
  summarising fetch; each of those 283 days was then re-read as raw text,
  with FindLaw's copy and the Internet Archive's capture of it tried, and
  19 were verified against it (Kentucky's six, North Dakota's five,
  Michigan's four, New Jersey's three and one of Massachusetts's). The
  other 264, whose pages refused a direct request (FindLaw's answering
  403, Utah's site failing its certificate check) and have no capture,
  were read through a summarising fetch, not verified against the text:
  they are carried as `Confidence::Approximate`, and their citations say
  so. A handful of rules were
  derived from wording relative to another day ("the Saturday before
  Memorial Day" as the Saturday on or before 29 May); each is in its
  row's rule. `crates/hc-holiday/tests/us_commemorative.rs` holds every
  one to its date in its first year and in 2026, worked out apart from the
  crate, and to its absence or gap the year before.

## Code

`crates/hc-holiday/src/countries/united_states.rs`: the federal rules
first in `US_RULES`, then the states', built by `state` and
`state_observance`, each with `.years` from its session law or
`.read_from` the year of its text, with `FRIDAY_AFTER_THANKSGIVING`, `ELECTION_DAY`,
`even_year_election_day`, `presidential_election_day`, Utah's
`TO_PRECEDING_OR_NEXT_MONDAY`, and `saturdays_except` with its three
Saturday rules for the half holidays; one region and one citation constant per
state, `US_AK` and `US_AK_LAW` to `US_WV` and `US_WV_LAW`. The
commemorative days are in the submodule `commemorative`, one citation
constant per state and site, joined to the rest in `US_ALL_RULES`. The
exchanges `XNYS` and `XNAS` keep their own lists. Anchors: the tests in
`crates/hc-holiday/tests/us_states.rs` and
`crates/hc-holiday/tests/us_commemorative.rs`, and the federal tests in
`crates/hc-holiday/tests/countries.rs`.
