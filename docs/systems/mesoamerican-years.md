# The Zapotec *yza*, the Mixtec year, and the Mesoamerican years not yet carried

Backs the identifiers `zapotec-yza` and `mixtec-year` in
`hc-calendars-regional`, and records why `purepecha-year` and `zoque-hame`
are still planned.
The Maya Haabʼ and the Aztec xiuhpohualli, which the *yza* is read against,
are in [mesoamerican-counts.md](mesoamerican-counts.md).

## What it is

In Urcid's summary of the Mesoamerican calendar, a people kept beside the
260-day count a vague year of eighteen twenty-day periods and five days
more, 365 in all, and named each year by a day of the 260-day count; the
day-names differ from language to language, but Caso found their order the
same in every list, the Mixe calendar's two transposed pairs apart
[urcid2001, pp. 82–85]. The two counts are called *piye* and *yza* in
Zapotec, and the 365-day one *cuiya* in Mixtec [marcus2000]; the Zoque called the two *may* and *hame*, and the
Purépecha the year *huriyata miucua* [wikipedia-mesoamerican-calendars].

**The Zapotec calendar.** Zapotec years were named by their year bearers
"throughout the corpus of Preclassic and Classic Zapotec hieroglyphic
inscriptions" [tavarez2008], but a year that can be placed on a Western
day comes from the end of the colonial period. Between September 1704 and
January 1705 the officials of more than forty Zapotec communities of the
alcaldía mayor of Villa Alta, in the Sierra Norte of Oaxaca, surrendered to the bishop's
representative the booklets their calendar specialists kept, in exchange
for immunity from idolatry proceedings. 106 texts survive, bound in 103
booklets, in the Archive of the Indies in Seville as AGI México 882; the
102 that copy out the 260-day calendar are "the largest single collection
of Mesoamerican calendars in existence" [tavarez2008]. Most of the
calendars list the 260 days from 1 Cayman; about half are followed by the
list of the 52 named years, from 1 Earthquake to 13 Soaproot; and several
annotate Zapotec days with Spanish dates, saints' feasts and weekdays
[tavarez2008]. Justeson and
Tavárez used every such annotation to correlate both counts with the
Gregorian calendar as it stood at Villa Alta at the end of the seventeenth
century [justeson2007, as tavarez2008 reports it]. The word for the
365-day period is spelled *yza* by Juan de Córdova's *Arte* of 1578 and in
the booklets alike, from proto-Zapotec *\*yiza* [tavarez2008, urcid2001];
Córdova does not describe the year's structure [tavarez2008].

**The Mixtec year.** The Mixtecs and the Popolocas named their years by
the same four signs as the Aztecs, Reed, Flint, House and Rabbit [caso1956,
pp. 494–495], but began the year with a different month:
"los mixtecos y popolocas principiaban su año por *Atemoztli*" [caso1967,
p. 78, citing jimenezmoreno1940, not read]. No Mixtec month name is known:
"Ni en las crónicas ni en los códices tenemos un solo glifo que con
seguridad podamos interpretar como correspondiente a un mes mixteco.
Ignoramos completamente los nombres de estos meses" [caso1956, p. 493].

## How it works

**The year.** Eighteen months of twenty days and a period of five, with no
intercalation: the Zapotec year of 1695 began on 23 February (Gregorian)
[tavarez2008, citing justeson2007: 28–30], and every year began 365 days
after the last. The Villa Alta specialists appear to have marked the new
year by the feast of Saint Matthias, 24 February, or its eve: the source
puts the feast on the first day "during 1689–1692" in one place and "from
1689 to 1691" in another, with the eve "from 1692 to 1695" [tavarez2008].
The dates the arithmetic gives are 24 February from 1689 to 1692 and
23 February from 1693 to 1695. The year's 183rd day was "the third day of
the tenth Zapotec month", and the confessions of 1704 speak of ceremonies
at the middle of the year [tavarez2008].

**The months.** The only list of Zapotec month names known is the solar
calendar that opens Booklet 85 (Manuscript 85), which Alcina Franch
published in 1966 and Urcid tabulates [urcid2001, Table 3.5, after
alcinafranch1966, p. 131]:

*toohua* is the first month of the year and *quicholla* the short period
at the end, the Zapotec counterpart of the Nahuatl *nemontemi*; the ninth,
*gogaa*, is Córdova's *cocij cogaa*, the "season of rain and wind" when no
field work is done, and fell in August [urcid2001, pp. 82–88]. As the
manuscript has it the year is irregular — twenty periods, not nineteen, a
first month of 18 days, a fourteenth of 19, a *quicholla* of 6 and a
twentieth of one day, 364 days in all by the table's figures. Alcina
Franch, Urcid reports, used the Christian equivalences written beside the
manuscript's calendar to explain and adjust some of the differences
[urcid2001, pp. 85–87; alcinafranch1966, pp. 131–32, not read].

**The year bearer.** A Zapotec year is named by the day of the 260-day
count **on which it begins** — not, as the Aztec year is, by a day near its
end — so the four year bearers are Earthquake, Wind, Deer and Soaproot,
the 17th, 2nd, 7th and 12th day-signs [tavarez2008; urcid2001, p. 83].
Since 365 = 260 + 105, and 105 is 1 more than a multiple of 13 and 5 more
than a multiple of 20, each year's bearer is one number higher and five
signs further on than the last's, and the four signs and thirteen numbers
come round together after 52 years [urcid2001, p. 83]. Tavárez and
Justeson give the colonial Northern Zapotec roots of the four as *xoo*,
*ee*, *china* and *biaa* [tavarez2008, Table 1]; in the manuscripts the
day's name also carries an augment that varies with its number, which
this library does not reproduce [tavarez2008, Table 2].

**The correlation.** The northern Zapotec 260-day count is "identical to
that proposed by Caso (1939 …) for the Mexica divinatory calendar of
Tlatelolco", apart from a possible difference in the hour at which the day
began, noon for the Zapotecs according to Córdova, and "the Zapotec year
began 63 days later than the Mexica year" [tavarez2008; caso1939,
cordova1578, not read]. That is the correlation `aztec-tonalpohualli`
already carries, and this library's `aztec-xiuhpohualli` opens its year 63
days before 23 February 1695, on 1 Izcalli, 22 December 1694; so the
Zapotec year begins on the xiuhpohualli's 64th day, 4 Tozoztontli, every
year, the two years having the same length. Which month opens Tavárez and
Justeson's Mexica year the 2008 paper does not say; the offset is exact
against Reingold and Dershowitz's Izcalli-first numbering, and the tests
hold it as a consequence, not as the anchor.

**Worked example: 23 August 1696.**

1. The anchor, 23 February 1695 (Gregorian), is fixed day 618 775, and
   the first day of year 0 here. 23 August 1696 is fixed day 619 322,
   547 days later.
2. 547 = 1 × 365 + 182: year 1, day 182 counted from 0. 182 ÷ 20 = 9
   remainder 2, so the tenth month, *gonaa*, and its third day: **3
   gonaa**, the 183rd day of the year, as Tavárez and Justeson say
   [tavarez2008].
3. Year 1 began 365 days after the anchor, on fixed day 619 140,
   23 February 1696. Under Caso's correlation 13 August 1521 (Julian),
   fixed day 555 403, is 1 Coatl, 104 days into its cycle, so fixed day
   619 140 is (619 140 − 555 403 + 104) mod 260 = 141 days into its own:
   number 141 mod 13 + 1 = 12, sign 141 mod 20 + 1 = 2, Wind. The year is
   **12 Wind**, *12 ee*, one on from 11 Earthquake as it should be.
4. The same arithmetic on 23 February 1695 gives (618 775 − 555 299) mod
   260 = 36: 11 and the 17th sign, **11 Earthquake**, the year Tavárez and
   Justeson name.

Because the year never takes a leap day and the Gregorian calendar does,
the first day of the Zapotec year slides a day earlier after each
29 February, and the 183rd day stays on 23 August from 1696 to 1703: the
source gives the range [tavarez2008], and the reason is that 1700 is not a
Gregorian leap year.

**The Mixtec year, as Caso reconstructs it.** Three statements on one page
of Caso fix it [caso1967, p. 78]. The months of the Mesoamerican peoples
agreed day for day, "con la única excepción que provenía de la colocación
de los *nemontemi*, que iban al fin del año"; the Mixtec year began with
Atemoztli; and the day that names a year is the "último del último mes".
So the Mixtec year runs Atemoztli, Tititl, Izcalli and on to
Panquetzaliztli, and then the five nemontemi. From Izcalli to
Panquetzaliztli its months are the Aztec ones day for day; its Atemoztli
and Tititl run five days behind the Aztec ones, because the Aztec
nemontemi come between Tititl and Izcalli and the Mixtec ones before
Atemoztli. Its year is named by the 260-day count on its 360th day, the
20th of Panquetzaliztli, which is also the 20th of the Aztec
Panquetzaliztli and forty days before the Aztec naming day, the 20th of
Tititl. Forty days is one more than a multiple of 13 and a multiple of 20,
so the Mixtec year is named by the same sign as the Aztec year that ends
forty days after it, with a number one lower, which is Milbrath's statement
of the rule [milbrath2022]. Caso's example: "el año de 1568 se llamó en el
Calendario popoloca-mixteco *10 Tecpatl* … por que el día *10 Tecpatl* fue
el último del mes *Panquetzaliztli*, último de su año que principiaba por
*Atemoztli*; pero el mismo día *10 Tecpatl*, también último de
*Panquetzaliztli*, no daba su nombre al año tenochca, que terminaba en
*Tititl*, con el día *11 Tecpatl*" [caso1967, p. 78].

Caso gives the year a name, not a Western day. The Western days here are
those of the Aztec correlation this library carries, Caso's as Reingold and
Dershowitz tabulate it ([mesoamerican-counts.md](mesoamerican-counts.md)),
which puts both of Caso's names where he puts them.

**Worked example: 1568.**

1. The xiuhpohualli year that contains 1568's naming days began on
   1 Izcalli, 13 January 1568 (Julian). Its 16th month, Panquetzaliztli,
   ends on its 320th day, 27 November 1568.
2. 27 November 1568 is fixed day 572 676. Its tonalpohualli count is
   (572 676 − 555 403 + 104) mod 260 = 217: number 217 mod 13 + 1 = 10,
   sign 217 mod 20 + 1 = 18, Tecpatl: **10 Tecpatl**, the Mixtec year's
   name.
3. Forty days later, on 6 January 1569, the 20th of the Aztec Tititl, the
   count is 257: number 257 mod 13 + 1 = 11, sign 257 mod 20 + 1 = 18:
   **11 Tecpatl**, the Tenochca year's name.
4. The Mixtec nemontemi follow 27 November, 28 November to 2 December, and
   the year named 10 Tecpatl ends on 2 December 1568. It began 365 days
   before 3 December 1568, on 4 December 1567, with 1 Atemoztli, which is
   the 6th of the Aztec Atemoztli.

**What the reconstruction is tested against.** Jiménez Moreno found that
this reading places the dates of the Cuilapan stone under the glyphs next
to them: in the Mixtec year 10 Flint, the day 11 Death "cae en el mes
Panquetzaliztli" under the banner that is that month's sign, and in the
year 10 Reed the days 11 Serpent and 6 Reed, nine days apart, fall in one
month, Tecuilhuitontli [caso1956, p. 494]. Caso is careful about one
point: the Codex Nuttall does not settle whether a Mixtec year began on the
day that names it, but if the two Reed-year days fall in one month, "el año
mixteco no pudo principiar por el día de su nombre" [caso1956, p. 495].
The reconstruction here names a year by its 360th day, the last of its
last month, and begins the next year six days after it, after the five
*nemontemi*: a year does not begin on its own naming day.

## What is carried

- **`zapotec-yza`** — the 365-day year, `ZapotecYzaCalendar` in
  `hc_calendars_regional::zapotec`. Its months are the nineteen positions
  *toohua* to *gohui* and *quicholla*, **eighteen of twenty days and a
  *quicholla* of five**, days counted from 1; `year` is the round, complete
  years since the one that began on 23 February 1695; the extra fields
  `year_bearer_number`, 1 to 13, and `year_bearer`, 1 to 4 for *xoo*,
  *ee*, *china* and *biaa*, name the year. `ZapotecYzaDate::year_bearer`
  gives the bearer as a 260-day position in the common order of the
  day-signs. Unbounded in both directions, with no leap year, as the
  Aztec and Maya years; `usage` unrecorded, because the sources read date
  the correlation to the 1680s and 1690s and the year bearers to the
  Preclassic and Classic inscriptions, and neither end of the year's use
  to a day.
- **The regular year is this library's reading of the manuscript.** The
  names and their order are Manuscript 85's; the lengths are not. Its
  first month of 18 days, its fourteenth of 19, its six-day *quicholla*
  and its one-day twentieth period *queainij* are not carried: a year of
  them would not be 365 days, and the year Tavárez and Justeson correlate
  is, with its 183rd day on the third of the tenth month.
- **The month names** are the calendar's own, in the lower-case spelling
  Urcid prints from Alcina Franch's transcription, and the `zap` locale in
  `hc-i18n` restates them with the four year bearers.
- **Not carried, and why.**
  - *The piye*, the Zapotec 260-day count, as a calendar: its day is the
    `aztec-tonalpohualli` day by the correlation above, and its names
    take an augment that changes with the number, which a single name
    per sign would misrepresent [tavarez2008, Table 2; urcid2001, Tables
    3.9–3.11].
  - *The noon day boundary* Córdova records: the library changes the
    day at midnight here, as in every Mesoamerican count it carries.
  - *Cline's synchrony.* Cline read Manuscript 85 as a Julian calendar of
    1696 and named that year 7 Motion [cline1975, as urcid2001 reports
    it], where Tavárez and Justeson make 1696 a Gregorian year, 12 Wind.
    No start day for Cline's year was read, so it cannot be registered as
    a second calendar; the disagreement is recorded here, and the
    library carries the later, fuller correlation.

- **`mixtec-year`** — `MixtecYearCalendar` in
  `hc_calendars_regional::mixtec`. Its months are the nineteen positions
  Atemoztli to Panquetzaliztli and the nemontemi, eighteen of twenty days
  and five; `year` is the round, complete years since the one that began on
  4 December 1567 (Julian), named 10 Tecpatl; the extra fields
  `year_bearer_number`, 1 to 13, and `year_bearer`, 1 to 4 for Acatl,
  Tecpatl, Calli and Tochtli, name the year. Unbounded both ways, with no
  leap year, as the Aztec year is; `usage` unrecorded.
- **The month names are the Aztec ones**, in the spelling of
  `aztec-xiuhpohualli`. They are the names of the Aztec months each Mixtec
  month coincides with, as Caso writes the Mixtec year; the Mixtec names
  are not known [caso1956, p. 493].
- **Not carried:** Milbrath's placing of the Aztec bearer on the first of
  Izcalli, the 341st day, rather than Caso's 360th [milbrath2022]; under it
  the Mixtec year would begin elsewhere, and it is not a second calendar
  here because no source read gives the Mixtec year that way. The Mixtec
  names of the day-signs, which Caso lists [caso1967] and Rodríguez Cano
  studies [rodriguezcano2008], are not carried: the bearers are named in
  Nahuatl, as Caso names them.

### The years still planned

- **`purepecha-year`.** Caso's chapter "El calendario de los tarascos"
  [caso1967, pp. 241–252], a corrected form of his article of 1943
  [caso1943], reconstructs the Tarascan year from the *Relación de
  Michoacán*: eighteen months of twenty days and five left over, the feasts
  on the last day of each month, and a table of the months in Julian dates
  for 1540–1543 anchored on the *Relación*'s Mazcoto, 7 June, which he
  identifies with Etzalcualiztli. What is still missing is three of the
  eighteen names: "En total tenemos 15 nombres para los meses tarascos …
  nos faltan únicamente tres" [caso1967, p. 245], and the table prints
  those months as *anónimo*. The reconstruction also has open choices: the
  year begins with the first unnamed month, on 9 April, in Caso's
  hypothesis and with Cuingo in Seler's; Curindaro could correspond to
  Tititl or to Panquetzaliztli, and Hunisperácuaro could fall on 20 March
  to 8 April or 15 March to 3 April, depending on where the five days go
  [caso1967, pp. 248, 252]; and the Tarascan 260-day count, and so a year
  bearer, is shown only indirectly [caso1967, pp. 249–250]. Paso y
  Troncoso's article [pasoytroncoso1887] was not read.
- **`zoque-hame`.** Nothing beyond the two names *may* and *hame*
  [wikipedia-mesoamerican-calendars, citing Edmonson in *Arqueología
  Mexicana* 41, of which only the online excerpt, with the same two terms,
  could be read]; Caso's *Los calendarios prehispánicos* has no Zoque
  chapter [caso1967]; Edmonson's *The Book of the Year* (1988) is
  lending-only on the Internet Archive; and no month list and no anchor
  were found in Lowe, Lee, Navarrete, Villa Rojas or Campbell and Kaufman in
  a form that could be read.

## Accuracy

Every count is integer arithmetic; the question is the anchor, and the
tests hold these published readings, all Gregorian:

| In a 10 Flint year 11 Death falls in Panquetzaliztli; in a 10 Reed year 11 Serpent and 6 Reed, nine days apart, fall in Tecuilhuitontli | [caso1956, p. 494] | `the_cuilapan_days_fall_in_the_months_of_their_glyphs` |
| Every Mixtec year ends forty days before the Aztec one, with the same sign one number lower; 1519 is Mixtec 13 Acatl | [milbrath2022] | `a_mixtec_year_is_the_aztec_one_forty_days_earlier_and_one_number_lower` |

Beyond the anchors the tests check that every day of a 52-year round
round-trips, through the fields too, that the year is 365 days with no
exception across 1600–2100, and that impossible positions are refused.

Two readings were not turned into tests. Tavárez and Justeson write that
the midpoint of the year that began in 1695 fell on 23 August 1696; the
183rd day of that year is 24 August 1695, and 23 August 1696 is the 183rd
of the next, which is the year their day counts for 28 and 29 August fit,
so the sentence mixes the two years, and the test holds the one the
arithmetic supports. And *gogaa*, the ninth month, falls from 1 to 20
August in 1696 here, which agrees with Urcid's "fell in August"
[urcid2001, p. 87] without testing much.

**The caveat, stated plainly.** The correlation rests on one corpus from
one district over some fifteen years, read by one pair of authors, and is
carried back and forward without limit, as the Aztec and Maya counts are.
Its 260-day half is established at Villa Alta on its own evidence, two
eclipses among it, and agrees with Caso's Mexica correlation; carried back
to the Classic inscriptions it is only as good as the assumption that the
count never broke. Its 365-day half is the Zapotec year of Villa Alta, which other
Zapotec regions need not have shared, and the months' lengths as the one
manuscript writes them are not the lengths carried. Cline's reading of the
same manuscript names 1696 differently.
