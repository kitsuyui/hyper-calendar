# i18n, m17n and L10n

This document describes how the library handles languages, scripts and
regional conventions. The three terms in the title are often used
interchangeably. Here they name three different jobs, and `hc-i18n` is
organised around them. `hc-i18n` carries 53 locales besides the root
(`hc_i18n::data::LOCALES`).

| Term | The job | Where it lives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **i18n** — internationalisation | Making the code capable of any language: no hard-coded English, no assumption of a 7-day week starting Monday, no assumption that a month name has one form | The API design of `hc-calendar` and `hc-format` |
| **m17n** — multilingualisation | Handling many languages *at once*, in the same document: mixed scripts, mixed directions, a Japanese era rendered inside an English sentence | `hc-i18n::direction`, the bidi isolation helpers, and the numbering systems |
| **L10n** — localisation | The actual data for a given locale: names, plural rules, first day of week | `hc-i18n::names` and `hc-i18n::plural`, as static tables; `hc-format` assembles them into patterns |

## The rule that makes it work

**Logic never contains a language.** Adding Welsh means adding a data entry.
Adding the Tibetan calendar's month names means adding a data entry. If a
change would put a `match locale { "tr" => ... }` into shared code, the
abstraction is wrong — see [policy.md](policy.md) §2.

The one exception is where the *rule itself* is linguistic: CLDR plural
rules are per-language algorithms. Those are expressed as data-driven
rule structures rather than as bespoke functions, so the shape stays uniform.

## Locales

`Locale` is BCP 47: language, optional script, optional region, plus the `-u-`
extension keys that matter for dates:

| Key | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `ca` | calendar | `ja-JP-u-ca-japanese` |
| `nu` | numbering system | `ar-EG-u-nu-arab` |
| `fw` | first day of week | `en-US-u-fw-sun` |
| `hc` | hour cycle | `en-GB-u-hc-h23` |

Fallback is an iterator: `ja-JP-u-ca-japanese` → `ja-JP` → `ja` → root. Lookups
walk it, so a partial data entry inherits the rest rather than failing. A
Chinese tag without a script first takes the one CLDR 48's likely subtags
give it, because the Chinese entries are `zh-Hans` and `zh-Hant`: `zh-TW` →
`zh-Hant-TW` → `zh-Hant` → `zh` → root, and `zh` → `zh-Hans` → `zh` → root.

Where a language is written in two scripts, both are carried and a tag
with no script takes the one CLDR 48's likely subtags give it: `pa` and

reach `yue-Hant` and `yue-CN` reaches `yue-Hans`, as `zh` does. Of the
regional variants, only European Portuguese is carried, because its file
states calendar words of its own (the weekdays *segunda* … *domingo* for
*seg.* … *dom.*, the quarters, the day periods, the Buddhist era); the
entry states only what `pt_PT.xml` states and inherits the rest from `pt`.
The other regional files — `en_GB.xml`, `es_419.xml`, `zh_Hant_HK.xml`,
`ur_IN.xml`, `ar_EG.xml` among them — are not carried, as the entries
above never carried them; a regional tag reaches its language.

The new entries are read out of their files [cldr48-most-spoken] by
following CLDR's own inheritance, not chosen by hand: every group of names the file states at
a release level (`approved` or `contributed`) is carried — each
calendar's months, the weekdays, the day periods, the quarters and each
calendar's eras, for the Gregorian, Buddhist, Japanese, Minguo, Hijri,
Hebrew, Coptic, Ethiopic, Persian and Indian national calendars and, for
Cantonese, the Chinese and Dangi — with its widths resolved through
`root.xml`'s aliases and the inheritance marker, as CLDR resolves them. A
group whose format names are root's is not carried: Telugu's Islamic
months, whose format names `te.xml` writes as the marker, so root's Latin
names, are left out, and so are the Cantonese files' numbered 1月 … for the
Persian, Coptic and Ethiopic months. Each also has its date templates, its
calendar names, its countries' names, its zones' exemplar cities and, in
`hc-humanize`, its relative-time phrases, all from the same file. What
CLDR does not give is not translated: `hc-humanize`'s hedges (*about*,
*nearly*) are root's language-free `~` and `<` for the new languages.

Measured on 2026-09-28 with rustc 1.98.1 in the `release-compact` profile,
the twelve locales grow the WebAssembly module's `calendars` layer from
1,007,231 to 1,057,231 bytes (5.0 %), its `holiday` layer, which carries
their country names, from 1,275,450 to 1,308,474 (2.6 %), and the `full`
layer from 2,679,300 to 2,820,532 (5.3 %); no other layer moves by more
than 0.1 %. They are not put behind a feature of their own, as the
exemplar cities are: `calendars` is the one layer that renders a date in a
locale, so a feature that left them out of it would leave their speakers
without the module, and `full` is by definition every layer at once. Their
exemplar cities are behind `localized-exemplar-cities` with every other
locale's.

## Numbering systems

A date is mostly digits, and not every locale writes digits the same way.
`hc_i18n::numbering` carries nine positional systems: `latn`, `fullwide`,
`arab` (Arabic-Indic), `arabext` (Persian), `deva`, `beng`, `mymr`, `thai`
and `hanidec`. It also carries four algorithmic Han systems, `hans`,
`hant`, `jpan` and `jpanfin`. Japanese era years need these: 令和元年, not
令和1年.

## Plural rules

A plural rule chooses the form of a word from a number. `en` needs two
forms. `ja` needs one. `ru` needs four. `ar` needs six. `cy` needs six with
different boundaries. So "3 days ago" cannot be a string template, and
`hc-humanize` chooses every form through these rules.

The CLDR operands (`n`, `i`, `v`, `w`, `f`, `t`) are implemented as defined, so
that 1.0 and 1 can take different forms where a language distinguishes them —
which several languages do.

## Names

Keyed by `(locale, calendar, width, context)`. The calendar is a registry
`CalendarId`, and each name list belongs to one of the cycles that calendar
declares about itself, so a nineteen-month calendar can be named and a
ten-day week has somewhere to live. [supported.md](supported.md) states
which calendars can be named and which cannot; the gap is measured rather
than discovered.

- **width**: wide, abbreviated, narrow, and the separate `short` weekday width
- **context**: `Format` (inside a date) versus `Standalone` (as a label). This
  distinction matters: Russian, Czech, Greek, Catalan and Finnish inflect the
  month name differently in the two positions.

Vocabulary shipped includes non-Gregorian systems: Hijri month names in Arabic
and English, Hebrew month names in Hebrew and English, Babylonian month names
in English, the Chinese calendar's months in Chinese, Japanese and Korean,
the Japanese lunisolar calendars' traditional months (睦月 … 師走, which
serve those calendars alone — a family's names never answer for another
family), and the zodiac animals in Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese
and English. The locales added for the most-spoken languages bring
CLDR's names for the other calendars their files name: the Hijri months in
Urdu, Marathi, Punjabi, Hausa and Cantonese, the Hebrew, Coptic, Ethiopic,
Persian and Indian national months in Urdu, Marathi, Telugu and Punjabi,
and the Hebrew and Indian national months and the Chinese and Dangi months
and zodiac in Cantonese. The ten stems and twelve
branches are not spelled here: `hc-calendar` catalogues their readings —
characters, pinyin, the Japanese kun and on readings, Hangul, quốc ngữ — and
each locale names the one it writes in.

Five locales are there so that a calendar can speak its own language when
asked for `native`:
Amharic (`am`) for the Ethiopic calendar, Coptic (`cop`) for the Coptic,
Burmese (`my`) for the Burmese, Tibetan (`bo`) for the Tibetan and Nepali
(`ne`) for the Bikram Sambat and Nepal Sambat. Their Gregorian vocabulary is
CLDR's where CLDR has the locale; `cop` has none and inherits. The
calendar-specific tables come from CLDR where it carries the calendar (the
Ethiopic months in `am`) and otherwise from the sources the calendar modules
cite — the Nepal Rajpatra for the Bikram Sambat months, the Wikipedia months
tables for the Coptic, Burmese and Nepal Sambat ones. A name no source
read gives is left empty rather than translated: the Ethiopic era names in
Amharic, the Coptic weekdays, the Burmese "Second Waso" prefix, the Tibetan
doubled-month prefix and sixty-year names, and either Nepali era.

A second group does the same for the Hindu, Mesoamerican, Southeast Asian
and Near Eastern calendars: Sanskrit (`sa`) and Hindi (`hi`) for the
lunisolar and Vikrami solar calendars, Tamil (`ta`), Malayalam (`ml`) and
Bengali (`bn`) for the regional solar calendars and the national one
(Tamil also for the Tamil year's sixty names, from the *Tamil Lexicon*),
Yucatec Maya (`yua`) and Nahuatl (`nah`) for the Maya and Aztec counts,
Zapotec (`zap`) for the Zapotec *yza*, Balinese (`ban`) and Javanese (`jv`) for the Pawukon and the pasaran,
Syriac (`syr`) for the Assyrian calendar, Kabyle (`kab`) and Standard
Moroccan Tamazight (`zgh`) for the Berber calendar, Mandaic (`mid`) for
the Mandaean, and Pashto (`ps`) for the Solar Hijri calendar as Afghanistan
kept it, whose Dari names the calendar declares itself and `fa-AF` reaches
through `fa`. CLDR has no `yua`, `nah`, `zap`, `ban` or `mid`, so those carry only
what the calendars' own sources say. Where CLDR carries an `indian`
calendar, its month names serve the national calendar and, in the same
words, the regional solar calendar (Tamil) or the lunisolar ones (Hindi);
the Kollam months come from Wikipedia's "Malayalam calendar", the Assyrian
months in Syriac from its "Assyrian calendar", the Mandaic weekdays from its
"Mandaean calendar". The Berber calendar takes CLDR's Kabyle and Tifinagh
Gregorian months, because the Encyclopédie berbère says the agrarian months
are the Julian ones under those names. Left empty for want of a source: the
rāśi names in Devanagari, the Sanskrit eras, the Mandaic months (the page
read has no Parwanaia), the Balinese and Javanese script forms, the Syriac
era, and the nakṣatras, which no calendar declares as a cycle. The
`hc-i18n` README tabulates what each of the twenty carries.

### A calendar's own language

A calendar states the languages its sources are written in
(`CalendarMeta::native_locales`, BCP 47, primary first: `he` for the
Hebrew calendar, `zh-Hans` and `zh-Hant` for the Chinese, `ja` for the
Japanese eras; empty for a day count, a proposal and the Gregorian
family). One rule serves every rendered cell. A renderer asked for a
locale that does not name a calendar falls to English, and when English
does not name it either, keeps the locale asked for with the calendar's own
names from its shape; it never borrows the calendar's own language for a
named locale, so a Japanese page shows the Umm al-Qura months in English,
not Arabic. Asked for `native`, it takes the calendar's own language first
where the crate carries it, then English.

### What a locale calls a calendar

A locale's name for a calendar (`hc_i18n::names::calendar_display_name`,
column 2 of `hc_calendars`) is CLDR 48's, and a calendar CLDR does not
name in the locale has none, so that a page falls back to the English
name the calendar declares (`CalendarMeta::english_name`, column 3). A
reader picks a calendar from a list by that name, so no two calendars may
share one — in English, in any locale, or asked for `native` —
and `crates/hyper-calendar/tests/distinct_names.rs` fails if two do.
Calendars that are one reckoning under competing conventions (policy §5)
are therefore named for the convention, in the words their module and
system document use:

| Calendars | Names |
| --- | --- |
| `maya-longcount`, `maya-longcount-gmt2`, `maya-longcount-584286` | Maya long count (GMT, 584283), (GMT+2, 584285), (Martin and Skidmore, 584286) |
| the tzolkʼin, haabʼ, Calendar Round and 819-day count under the same three | Maya tzolk'in, Maya haab, Maya Calendar Round, Maya 819-day count, each with the same three qualifiers |
| `persian`, `persian-arithmetic` | CLDR's `persian` name for `persian`; for `persian-arithmetic` the same name followed by "(2820)", the length of Birashk's cycle — "Persian Calendar (2820-year cycle)" in English |

GMT is the initialism of Goodman–Martínez–Thompson and stays as the
literature writes it. A qualifier is never translated: where CLDR gives
the base name and no source gives a qualified form, the locale's name
carries a qualifier that needs no translation, a number in ASCII digits,
in parentheses as that locale's own CLDR names write one. The other
families of variants — the Julian–Gregorian reforms, the Hijri
conventions, the Tibetan versions, the Solar Hijri readings, the Hindu
solar calendars, the Japanese courts — already name what distinguishes
them in English, and where CLDR names more than one of a family, as it
does the four Hijri conventions, its names already differ.

### What a locale calls a country

A locale's name for a country (`hc_i18n::territories::territory_name`,
column 3 of `hc_holiday_tables`) is CLDR 48's
`localeDisplayNames/territories`, the plain value at the `approved` and
`contributed` levels, as for calendar names: 日本 in `ja`, Deutschland in
`de`, and in English `Hong Kong SAR China`, not the `alt="short"` form.
Only the 195 regions `hc-holiday` keeps a country's table for are
carried — every country an exchange's table takes its days off from is
one of them — and only for the carried locales whose CLDR file names
them: 47 of the 53. Kabyle lacks six of the 195 and Tamazight three,
Tibetan and Sanskrit name ten, Punjabi in the Arabic script names only
Pakistan, as `pa_Arab.xml` does, and European Portuguese names the 38 that
`pt_PT.xml` names apart from `pt.xml` and takes the rest from `pt`'s table;
Coptic, whose every value is unconfirmed, and the five locales CLDR has no
file for have no table. A
country a locale does not name is named as CLDR's English names it,
never in another language, so that the tag `en` stands for one name of a
country wherever it answers; an exchange, a tradition and a
set of observances are always named in English, since CLDR names none and
the library does not translate them. `hc_holiday_tables` says which
answered in its locale-used column.

The `alt="short"` values are carried beside the plain ones
(`hc_i18n::territories::short_name`, column 8 of `hc_holiday_tables`),
from the same files at the same levels: `Hong Kong` for `HK` and `UK` for
`GB` in English, 香港 in Japanese. CLDR shortens few names — among the 195,
`BA`, `GB`, `HK`, `KR`, `MM`, `MO`, `PS`, `SA` and `US`, and no locale all
of them — so the column is empty for most countries. It is looked up in
the file that gave the plain name, without walking the fallback chain,
and is empty where that file writes the short value as the inheritance
marker `↑↑↑`, which resolves to the plain name itself, and where the plain
name is the table's own English one rather than CLDR's.

The names are about 135 kilobytes of text, so they are behind
`hc-i18n`'s `territories` feature, which only the facade's `holiday`
feature turns on: the WebAssembly module's `calendars` layer, which names
no country, does not carry them, and its `holiday` layer grows by about
11 % (1,020,159 to 1,136,359 bytes on 2026-09-26); the twelve tables of
the most-spoken languages' locales added 2.6 % more (1,275,450 to
1,308,474 bytes on 2026-09-28). Each table is one
string of names in code order, so that the nine thousand names do not
cost eight bytes of slice each on `wasm32`; the source keeps every name
beside its code, and a macro checks the codes at compile time.

### What a locale calls a zone's city

A zone's exemplar city (`hc_i18n::exemplar_cities::exemplar_city`,
column 6 of `hc_zones` and `hc_zone_location`) is CLDR 48's
`dates/timeZoneNames/zone/exemplarCity` at the `approved` and
`contributed` levels: 東京 in `ja` for `Asia/Tokyo`, Wien in `de` for
`Europe/Vienna`. Only the 418 names `hc-tz` gives a location are carried,
each read under CLDR's own identifier for the zone, which for 19 of them
is an older IANA name (`Asia/Calcutta` for `Asia/Kolkata`). English is
`en.xml`'s value, else `root.xml`'s, else the name UTS #35 derives from
the zone's identifier, its last field with underscores as spaces
(`America/Los_Angeles` is `Los Angeles`). Where a locale's file writes the
inheritance marker `↑↑↑`, as German does for `Europe/Berlin`, the locale
answers with the root name under its own tag; where it has no value, the
city is English's, with `en` in the locale-used column. Forty-one locales
besides English have values; Tibetan, Sanskrit, Tamazight and Punjabi in
the Arabic script have none, and every Coptic and Kabyle value is
unconfirmed. European Portuguese's parent is `pt`, not root, so where
`pt_PT.xml` writes the marker its table has no line and the lookup goes on
to `pt`, which answers under its own tag.

English is about 2 kB and comes with the facade's `tz` feature. The other
locales are about 230 kB of text, behind `hc-i18n`'s
`localized-exemplar-cities` feature, which the WebAssembly module's
`calendars` layer turns on: a build with `tz` alone writes every city in
English, and one with `tz` and `calendars` in the locale. Measured on
2026-09-27, the `tz` layer is 92,831 bytes with the English cities and
would be 274,513 with every locale's, three times as large, which is why
the other locales are not in it; with the twelve locales of the
most-spoken languages it would be 337,703 (2026-09-28, against 97,478
with the English cities). `docs/systems/zone-locations.md` explains
the lookup.

### What a locale calls an almanac note

The Japanese almanac's notes (`hc_i18n::almanac`, column 3 of
`hc_almanac_day`) are named in two languages. Japanese names them as the
almanacs print them, which are `hc-almanac`'s own names, compared with the
National Astronomical Observatory's 暦Wiki and the National Diet
Library's 「日本の暦」. English writes them in their Hepburn romanisation,
as English Wikipedia's "Rokuyō" writes the six days, and names the
twenty-eight mansions by the asterisms' English names, Horn to Chariot.
A locale's chain is walked as for a country's name; a note it does not
name is English's, and a note English does not name — the modern
combinations, which have no reading — is Japanese's, with the tag of the
table that answered in column 4. `native` asks for Japanese first.
Chinese and Korean almanacs name some of the same cycles, but no source
for their names was read, so no Chinese or Korean name is carried. The sexagenary day is in the
locale's reading of the cycle, like the sexagenary year of a date.

### What a locale calls a horizon

A horizon's name (`hc_i18n::horizons`, column 6 of `hc_horizons`) is
carried only where a source in the language names the convention: for
Japanese, the National Astronomical Observatory's own wording; for another
language, its national observatory's or almanac office's wording, or the
title of the convention's own source in it. None is translated. So only
`usno` has names, the Hong Kong Observatory's 美國海軍天文氣象台 and
美国海军天文气象台 in `zh-Hant` and `zh-Hans` and the IMCCE's "Observatoire
naval de Washington D.C." in `fr`; every other locale, Japanese, Korean,
German, Spanish, Arabic, Persian and Hebrew among them, writes all three
horizons in English, with `en` in column 7.

### What a locale calls a reckoning

The terms of the other reckonings of a day and a year
(`hc_i18n::reckonings`) — the choghadiya of `hc_choghadiya`, the Panchak
kinds of `hc_panchak`, the Kumbh sites and Pushkaram rivers of `hc_kumbh`
and `hc_pushkaram`, the planets of `hc_planetary_hour`, the night watches
of `hc_night_watch`, and the folk days of `hc_folk_day` — are each named in
the one language their source writes them in, with the crates' own names:
English for the Indian terms, Drik Panchang's, Prokerala's and India
TV's, the Mela Adhikari's and Wikipedia's, and for the planets; `zh-Hant`
for 一更 to 五更; `zh-Hans` for 入梅, 出梅 and the counts of the first
month; `vi` for Tam Nương and Nguyệt Kỵ; and `tr` for the halves and the
named days of the Turkish folk year. A locale's chain is walked as for an
almanac note; a term it does not name is English's, and a term English
does not name is its own language's, with the tag of the table that
answered beside the name. `native` asks for the term's own language first.
No term has a name in a second language, since none was read, and none is
translated; the vocabulary test holds every name to the crate that
computes the term.

### Templates

Each locale states how it writes a year with its era, a day and a whole
date, as templates over placeholders (`{era}{year}年`, `{month} {day},
{year}`), and an entry serving a calendar family states what differs for
that family: the Chinese calendar's year by its stem and branch and its
days by their Han names. `hc-format` renders them. Every template names
the CLDR pattern it was read from, and a locale that has stated none gets
the fields in order; no orthography is invented.

A calendar whose sources write its dates in a notation of their own,
whatever the language, states it once, in `hc_i18n::notation`, as a
level between the entries that serve it and the locale's general
templates: the Long Count's `13.0.13.17.8`, the ISO week date
`2026-W39-7`, the Tzolkʼin's `1 Lamat`, the Thai lunar date's `แรม 1 ค่ำ
เดือนสิบ`. Each notation cites the source that writes dates so.

### A date's extra fields

A calendar's extra fields — the Long Count's places, the Hindu year's
name among sixty, the Julian Day Number a day count is kept against —
have identifiers, `baktun`, `samvatsara`, `julian-day-number`, which are
keys and never text. A template writes one only where the calendar's
sources write the date with it, as `{extra:FIELD}`: the name of its value
where the field's values are named, else its number. There is no
placeholder for all of them, so a formatted date never holds a
`name=value` pair; the rest are metadata. `hc_i18n::fields` labels every
field a registered calendar sets, in English, in the words its system
document uses, and says which cycle, if any, names a field's values; the
lines of `hc_day_extras` carry the identifier, the value, the label, the
named value and whether the date already writes it. A label in another
language is carried only where a source in that language names the
field, and none is yet.

### A calendar's own names

Some names belong to a calendar rather than to a language. The haabʼ month
*Pop*, the Pawukon week *Dungulan*, the Darian month *Sagittarius* are the
same in every locale that has not coined its own word, and a locale that has
none should not have to transcribe them to be able to print a date. So a
calendar declares those names with its shape
(`hc_calendar::shape::CycleShape::named`), and `hc_i18n::names::position_name`
resolves a position by asking the locale first and the calendar second. The
locale's entry is an override, not a requirement.

The rule for what goes where: a name is the calendar's when its sources use
one orthography and languages borrow it; it is the locale's when each language
has its own word, as every language does for the Gregorian months. A cycle
whose positions are numbered — the tzolkʼin's thirteen, a lunisolar
calendar's months — declares no names at all.

An era's name (`hc_i18n::names::era_label`, column 4 of `hc_describe_day`
and the `{era}` of every formatted date) follows one rule that ends at a
name: the locale's; for an era several calendars count
(`data::SHARED_ERAS`), the name the locale gives it in the calendar that
names it for all of them, Hindi's शक for the Śaka years of `hindu-lunar`;
else the calendar's own, 嘉永 or *Kaei*; else English's, `Minguo` under
`ja`. An era code — `roc`, `os`, `saka` — is an identifier, and the
vocabulary test holds that no rendered date in any carried locale writes
one.

### Names a law gave for a period

A state has sometimes renamed the months by law, and the new names held
only while the law did. Turkmenistan did so in 2002 and returned to the old
names on 1 July 2008. `hc_i18n::dated` carries such a period beside a
locale's vocabulary: the language, the calendar, the names and the days.
`dated::period_on` says which names a locale writes on a day, and answers
`Undecided` where the sources do not date the change to the day. For
Turkmenistan the vote of 8 August 2002 is dated and the day the law took
effect is not, so 8 August to 31 December 2002 is undecided and 2003 is
the first year in force. No locale for Turkmen is carried, so outside the
period the fallback chain supplies the names.

## Direction and bidi

Arabic, Hebrew, Persian and Urdu are right-to-left, and a date embedded in the
opposite direction needs isolation or it renders scrambled. `hc-i18n::direction`
reports script direction and supplies the U+2066–U+2069 isolates a formatter
needs. This is the m17n half of the job: not "can we render Arabic" but "can we
render Arabic *inside* English without either one breaking".

## Casing

Turkish dotless i and German noun capitalisation mean that "uppercase the first
letter" is locale-dependent. `hc-i18n::casing` keeps that out of the formatter.

## Adding a locale

1. Add a `LocaleData` entry: the cycle names the language has its own words
   for (a calendar's own names are the fallback), weekday names, day
   periods, eras, quarters, first day of week.
2. Add its plural rule if the language is not already covered.
3. The consistency tests check that each name list is as long as the cycle it
   names, that no name is empty or padded, that the numbering system exists
   and that the tag is canonical.

A locale needs no code change.
